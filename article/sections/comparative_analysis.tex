\section{Comparative Analysis}
\label{sec:comparative}

This section synthesises the evidence from Sections~\ref{sec:sensing} and~\ref{sec:sp} into a structured comparison of sensing and signal processing method combinations along four dimensions: sensitivity to specific lubrication fault types, sensitivity to operating conditions, practical deployment requirements, and technology maturity. The goal is to
\begin{itemize}
    \item characterise the trade-offs that determine which combination is appropriate for a given deployment context and
    \item identify gaps and most promising directions for industrial applications.
\end{itemize}

% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{Sensitivity to Lubrication Fault Type}
\label{subsec:comp_fault}

Three principal lubrication fault types are of interest in bearing LCM as described in Section~\ref{sec:lubrication_physics}: starvation, contamination, and viscosity degradation. These faults produce partially distinct signal signatures, which determines which SP methods are most applicable to each. A large part of the reviewed literature, however, does not target these fault types directly but instead quantifies film-thickness inadequacy through the viscosity ratio $\kappa$ or the specific film thickness $\Lambda$, typically by varying speed and temperature on a fresh lubricant charge. This is a distinct condition a the lubricant is neither starved, contaminated, nor aged, but the operating point yields an insufficient film. It is therefore reported as a separate column rather than folded into starvation or viscosity degradation. Several cross-cutting observations emerge from Table~\ref{tab:fault_sensitivity}. First, only one combination reports high sensitivity to all three fault types simultaneously: the CycleGAN-based deep learning pipeline of Zhang et al.\ \cite{zhang_interpretable_2026} applied to vibration. Its field-domain performance rests on generated rather than measured anomaly examples, so the coverage is not yet demonstrated on measured field faults. Among combinations evaluated entirely on measured data, AE with time-domain features has the broadest coverage -- starvation, contamination, and $\kappa$/$\Lambda$ estimation -- but its contamination sensitivity is selective for abrasive hard particles (as demonstrated by the Delft group for AE hit-rate), and its early-stage contamination performance is established only relative to vibration, stator current, and shock pulse rather than in absolute terms \cite{tandon_condition_2007}. Active ultrasonic and electrical methods provide quantitative film thickness estimates and are therefore inherently sensitive to both starvation and viscosity degradation through their direct relationship with $h_m$, although this sensitivity is inferred from the measured quantity rather than demonstrated on those fault types directly, and neither method detects debris contamination. Magnetic, inductive, and in-line optical methods target debris directly and provide complementary coverage for contamination monitoring, but all require access to the lubricant flow and are inapplicable to grease-lubricated or sealed bearings.

A further group of studies detects lubrication degradation without resolving which fault type produced it. Zhang et al.\ \cite{zhang_graph-data-based_2024} detect lubrication abnormality from fused vibration, AE, and temperature features, but report performance over a degradation process rather than against an induced fault type. The corresponding entry in Table~\ref{tab:fault_sensitivity} is therefore placed under the fault types assumed to dominate that process, and is marked accordingly. Such studies establish that a degradation signal is detectable without establishing what the sensor is responding to, which limits their use for method selection.

Second, passive methods are most consistently sensitive to starvation, the fault type that generates the greatest increase in asperity contact energy. The viscosity degradation column, by contrast, is almost entirely empty, and this reflects a gap in the literature rather than a demonstrated insensitivity. No reviewed study subjects a bearing to thermally or oxidatively aged lubricant and reports the resulting sensor response. The studies that appear to address viscosity instead vary the operating viscosity through speed and temperature on a fresh lubricant charge, which shifts $\kappa$ without degrading the lubricant itself -- the reason these results are reported in the $\kappa$/$\Lambda$ column. Whether a sensor calibrated to track $\kappa$ under speed and temperature variation responds equivalently to an aged lubricant at the same nominal $\kappa$ remains untested, and this gap is carried forward to Section~\ref{sec:gaps}.

This asymmetry plausibly reflects how the fault types are induced experimentally rather than their relative industrial importance. Starvation is realised directly and repeatably within a single test session by withholding or removing lubricant, and an inadequate $\kappa$ follows immediately from a chosen speed and temperature. Thermal and oxidative aging, by contrast, develop over hundreds to thousands of operating hours, and the resulting degradation state is harder to control and to verify than a lubricant quantity. The evidence base is therefore weighted toward the fault types that are inexpensive to induce, which is consistent with the scarcity of long-duration run-to-degradation datasets discussed in Section~\ref{subsec:gap_datasets}.

Third, the temporal response of thermal monitoring is qualitatively different from acoustic methods: temperature rise is a consequence of sustained elevated friction and therefore lags the onset of inadequate lubrication by minutes to hours, whereas AE and vibration respond on the timescale of individual asperity contacts (microseconds to milliseconds). Thermal monitoring is therefore better suited to detecting the existence of inadequate lubrication conditions than to detecting their onset or intermittent occurrence.

The fundamental difficulty for passive methods is that the same signal change -- an increase in RMS, AE energy, or ultrasound dB level -- can be produced by an increase in speed, load, or temperature as well as by worsening lubrication. Active methods (electrical, ultrasonic reflectometry) provide more direct film thickness measurements and are inherently less speed- and load-dependent, though they remain sensitive to temperature through lubricant viscosity. The $\kappa$ regression approach of Jakobsen et al.\ \cite{jakobsen_vibration_2021,jakobsen_detecting_2023} is noteworthy as a principled strategy for passive methods: by training regression models with $\kappa$ as the target -- a variable that already encodes the speed, load, and viscosity dependence through the Hamrock--Dowson and ISO 281 models -- the learned model implicitly compensates for these operating condition effects.

Passive non-invasive methods (AE, vibration, ultrasound, sound, thermal) share the critical practical advantage that they require no bearing modification and can in principle be retrofitted to bearings in service. The trade-off is that their signals carry lubrication information mixed with other sources (machine vibration, electromagnetic interference, structural resonances), requiring more sophisticated SP to extract the lubrication-relevant component. Active and invasive methods provide cleaner, more direct lubrication information at the cost of installation complexity and, in some cases, bearing modification.

The installation constraints in Table~\ref{tab:deployment} highlight a practical hierarchy among passive non-invasive methods. AE is the most installation-sensitive: the frequency-dependent attenuation described in Section~\ref{subsec:transmission} means that signal quality degrades significantly if the sensor is separated from the bearing by bolted joints, material transitions, or long structural paths. A sensor poorly positioned relative to the bearing can render high-frequency AE content undetectable regardless of its nominal sensitivity. Passive ultrasound at 20--100\,kHz is more tolerant of imperfect mounting, which partly explains its wider industrial adoption. Vibration accelerometers, operating at yet lower frequencies, are the most robust to mounting position variations. Thermal sensors, operating at DC, are entirely insensitive to structural transmission path effects. Among passive methods, AE imposes the highest data-rate requirement ($>$500\,kHz sampling) due to the high-frequency nature of the emitted waves, which creates storage and computation challenges for continuous real-time monitoring. The two-dimensional amplitude-energy framework of Hou and Zhang \cite{hou_-situ_2025} demonstrates a computationally demanding offline analysis; real-time implementations would require significant simplification or dedicated hardware.

A striking observation is the large gap between the industrial deployment of passive ultrasound threshold methods and the research-stage status of virtually all more sophisticated SP approaches. The threshold method -- despite its well-documented limitations (speed-load confounding, no degradation modelling) -- is the only passive non-invasive LCM method in routine commercial deployment. All regression, ML, and physics-informed SP methods remain at the research stage, with none yet validated in a full industrial deployment at scale. This gap constitutes a primary motivation for the research direction described in Section~\ref{sec:gaps}.

\subsection{Summary of Comparative Findings}
\label{subsec:comp_summary}

The comparative analysis reveals a structural inversion at the core of the LCM field: the sensing methods most accessible for industrial deployment -- passive, non-invasive, retrofittable without bearing modification -- are supported by the least mature signal processing, while the methods with the most physically grounded and quantitatively capable SP (active ultrasonic reflectometry, electrical circuit models) impose installation barriers that restrict them to controlled test environments or high-value dedicated assets. Industrial deployment of passive LCM is consequently dominated by threshold methods developed in the early 2000s, while the diagnostically superior SP approaches -- physics-informed $\kappa$ regression, multi-sensor GNN fusion -- remain at the research stage, validated only under constant operating conditions. Closing this gap requires SP methods that are simultaneously physically interpretable, robust to operating condition variation, and deployable on standard industrial hardware without bearing modification -- the challenge that defines the research gaps identified in Section~\ref{sec:gaps}.
