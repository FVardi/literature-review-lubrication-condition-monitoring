\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This review has provided an examination of signal processing methods for lubrication condition monitoring of ball bearings. To the author's knowledge it is the first review in the LCM literature to organise its findings by signal processing method family and sensing modality, and to give special attention to passive methods.

On the sensing side, the review establishes a two-axis classification -- active/passive and invasive/non-invasive -- and shows that passive, non-invasive methods (acoustic emission, vibration, passive ultrasound, microphone arrays\todo{Resolve with the todo in the signal processing part}) have been demonstrated to carry genuine lubrication-relevant information. These signals also respond to speed, load, and temperature independently of lubrication state. Active and invasive methods (ultrasonic reflectometry, electrical capacitance/impedance, surface acoustic waves) provide more direct film thickness information via physics-based models, at the cost of installation complexity and, in some cases, bearing modification.

On the signal processing side, the master cross-reference matrix (Table~\ref{tab:sp_crossref}) reveals a wide diversity of methods applied to LCM, spanning from computationally trivial time-domain statistics (RMS, pulse count rate) to sophisticated methods (graph neural networks, $\kappa$ regression). Time-domain statistical features are prevalent in practical and industrial applications due to their simplicity and interpretability. Frequency-domain methods isolate lubrication-related frequency content that time-domain methods mix with other sources. Physics-based model-driven methods (spring-layer acoustic model, electrical circuit models, $\kappa$/$\Lambda$ regression targets) bridge signal processing and lubrication physics, enabling quantitative film thickness estimation from sensor signals. Machine learning and data fusion methods show diagnostic promise but are at the research stage.

The comparative analysis in Section~\ref{sec:comparative} reaches four principal conclusions. First, coverage of fault types is uneven: starvation and film-thickness inadequacy are the most frequently tested categories for passive methods, viscosity degradation has been addressed in a single study, and most results rest on one laboratory study each, so the methods cannot be compared and ranked. Second, multiple studies with passive methods set the lubrication indicator through speed and temperature and hold load constant, so lubrication and operating-condition effects are rarely separated. Third, active methods measure film thickness directly rather than inferring it, at the cost of installation complexity. Fourth, the maturity gap between research and industry is large: threshold methods are the only passive approach found in commercial products, while all more advanced SP approaches remain at the research or prototype stage.

Four signal-processing-centric research gaps are identified. The most consequential are the absence of standardised benchmark datasets that would enable rigorous cross-study comparison and the unsolved problem of speed- and load-invariant feature extraction for passive methods. Additional gaps are the lack of multi-sensor fusion validation in realistic industrial environments and the unexplored potential of self-supervised learning to reduce the need for labelled LCM data.

The overarching conclusion is that the signal processing layer, for passive, non-invasive methods, is the main barrier limiting LCM from laboratory demonstration to industrial deployment. Advanced sensors capable of detecting lubrication-relevant signals exist and are demonstrated. What is lacking are standardised datasets which allow isolation of lubrication conditions and SP methods that are simultaneously physically interpretable, robust to operating condition variation, and validated under industrial conditions.
